Masked Language Modeling

Q01

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q01 Why are masked tokens used in encoder-based training objectives?

Masking creates a self-supervised training signal that forces the model to use bidirectional context to understand each token, which

is exactly the capability needed for downstream understanding

tasks.

PointWhat it means
Creating a supervised signal without labelsTraining a supervised model requires labeled data, which is expensive. Masking allows you to create effectively unlimited training examples from raw text with no human annotation. Mask "bank" in "The river bank was steep" and the label is "bank". You can do this for any text corpus.
Forcing deep contextual understandingTo predict the masked token correctly, the model must use all available surrounding context. For the sentence "She played the [MASK] at the concert last night", the model needs to understand that the context suggests a musical instrument, and concert context makes "violin", "piano", or "guitar" likely.

This requires genuine semantic understanding of the sentence, not

just local n-gram statistics.

PointWhat it means
What the model learnsThrough billions of these predictions during training, the model learns rich contextual representations where each token's meaning is shaped by its full context. This is exactly what makes BERT representations useful for downstream tasks: they encode contextual meaning, not just surface statistics.
The 80-10-10 trickOriginal BERT uses an interesting modification: 80% of selected tokens are replaced with [MASK], 10% are replaced with a random token, 10% are left unchanged. The random and unchanged tokens prevent the model from learning to only update representations when it sees [MASK].

It must maintain good representations for all tokens since any could be the prediction target.

SECTION 02 · 1 QUESTION

Attention Scaling

Q02

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q02 Why is the attention score scaled by the square root of the key dimension?

This is one of those details that seems minor but has a real and important effect on training.

WHAT HAPPENS WITHOUT SCALING

The attention score for token i attending to token j is the dot product of

query vector i and key vector j.

If the key dimension is d_k, and the vectors have values roughly normally distributed with mean 0 and variance 1, then the dot product has variance

d_k (it is a sum of d_k multiplied random variables).

When d_k is large, say 64 or 128, the raw dot products can be quite large in

magnitude, like 30 or 50. Softmax of values like (-50, -30, 50, 30) is essentially (0, 0, 1, 0). The distribution collapses to a one-hot vector.

PointWhat it means
Why that is a problemWhen softmax produces values very close to 0 or 1, its gradient is nearly zero. The model learns very slowly or stops learning. You want softmax to produce a range of intermediate values so gradients flow through all attention connections, not just the argmax.
The fixDivide by square root of d_k. This normalizes the variance of the dot products back to approximately 1 regardless of dimension. Softmax operates in a range where it produces meaningful distributions and gradients are healthy.

Concretely: if d_k equals 64, divide scores by 8. If d_k equals 128,

divide by roughly 11.3. As models get wider with larger head dimensions, the scaling factor grows accordingly.

Depth & Layer Hierarchy

Q03

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q03 Why are transformer layers stacked repeatedly, and how does depth influence model capability?

The stacking is not just about having more parameters. Each layer plays a qualitatively different role in the hierarchy of

representations.

WHAT EACH LAYER LEARNS

Early layers tend to learn syntactic patterns: is this a noun or a verb? What

are the grammatical dependencies between these words? Middle layers start learning more semantic patterns: what is the meaning of this phrase in

context? Later layers capture task-relevant, abstract representations: what

is the sentiment of this sentence?

What is the answer to this question?

We know this from attention visualization and probing experiments. If you

train a simple classifier on the representations at each layer to predict

something like POS tags, early layers are best for POS tagging. Later layers

are best for semantic roles. The hierarchy is real and meaningful.

PointWhat it means
Why stacking worksEach layer's output becomes the next layer's input. Layer 2 does not operate on raw token embeddings. It operates on the enriched representations that layer 1 produced. This compositionality is what makes deep networks powerful. Complex patterns are built from simpler patterns in a hierarchy.
Depth vs width trade-offYou could make a single layer much wider (more neurons in the FFN, larger attention dimension). But empirically, depth beats width for the same parameter budget. A deep, moderately wide network generalizes better than a shallow, very wide network. The hierarchical processing that depth enables appears to match the hierarchical structure of language itself.

Practically, modern LLMs typically have 32 to 96 layers. GPT-3 has 96

layers. Each layer contributes meaningfully to the final representation.

Encoder & Decoder Mechanics

Q04, Q05

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q04 Why do decoder architectures use masked self-attention while encoders do not?

The masking is not a design preference. It is a strict requirement for autoregressive generation to work correctly.

WHY MASKING IS NECESSARY FOR GENERATION

When a decoder generates text, it produces one token at a time. At position

3, it generates token 3. At this point, tokens 4, 5, 6 do not exist yet. If the

model attended to future positions during generation, it would be using information that is unavailable in practice.

More critically, if during training we let the model attend to future tokens, it

would trivially solve the prediction task by just copying from ahead. The

model would learn nothing useful because it can always see the answer.

HOW MASKING IS IMPLEMENTED

During training, all tokens are present simultaneously (for efficiency). The

mask is applied as a large matrix that sets all upper-triangle attention scores to negative infinity before the softmax. This makes the attention

weights for future positions exactly zero.

The model is forced to predict each position using only past positions, even though the entire sequence is technically present in memory.

WHY ENCODERS DO NOT NEED MASKING

Encoders are used for understanding, not generation. You give the model a

complete input and ask it to produce a representation. There is no

generation step where future tokens would be unavailable. Seeing the full

bidirectional context is actually beneficial and produces better representations.

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q05 Why do encoder-based models often require pooling mechanisms for downstream tasks?

Encoder models produce one representation vector per input token. Most downstream tasks need a single vector representing the entire

input. Pooling is the bridge.

THE MISMATCH PROBLEM

Say you want to classify a 50-token sentence as positive or negative

sentiment. BERT gives you 50 vectors, each representing a token's contextual meaning. But a classifier needs one vector as input. How do you

go from 50 to 1?

That is what pooling solves. It aggregates across the sequence

dimension to produce a fixed-size representation.

PointWhat it means
Why this design existsBERT-style encoders were designed to produce rich per-token representations because they were trained on token-level tasks (MLM predicts individual masked tokens). The model was not explicitly trained to produce sequence-level representations. Pooling is the adapter that converts token-level representations to sequence-level representations for downstream classification, similarity, and retrieval tasks.
Why it matters for embedding modelsWhen you use a model like sentence-BERT to produce a semantic embedding for a document, you are applying pooling over the encoder's token representations. The choice of pooling method directly affects the quality of the resulting embedding, which in turn affects retrieval quality in a vector search system.

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q07 Why has RoPE become common in modern LLM architectures?

Several practical advantages have made RoPE the dominant choice in post-2022 architectures.

PointWhat it means
Better length generalizationAs I mentioned, RoPE's relative position formulation allows the model to handle sequences longer than those seen during training more gracefully than additive sinusoidal encoding. This is crucial because:
Training on very long sequences is expensiveProduction use cases often need longer context than what was practical to train on

Users want to be able to process long documents without the

model falling apart

COMPATIBILITY WITH KV CACHING

In inference, key and value vectors are cached as context grows (this is the

KV cache that makes autoregressive generation efficient). RoPE applies the

rotation to keys and queries in a way that is compatible with incremental KV

cache updates.

The positional rotation can be applied at the time of caching and

correctly interacts with later queries.

PointWhat it means
Scalable context extensionTechniques like YaRN, LongRoPE, and various other RoPE scaling methods allow models to extend their context window after training with relatively small amounts of fine-tuning. A LLaMA-2 model trained on 4k context has been extended to 128k context using RoPE interpolation techniques. This flexibility is very valuable practically.
It just works well empiricallyPerformance on benchmarks that test long-context understanding is consistently better with RoPE than with additive positional encoding, which has driven rapid adoption.
SECTION 07 · 1 QUESTION

Mixture of Experts Transformers

Q08

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q08 What challenges arise when training MoE transformer architectures?

MoE training introduces complications that do not exist in dense model training, and several of them are tricky to get right.

EXPERT COLLAPSE

Without load balancing, gradient descent finds a shortcut: always route to

the 1-2 experts that perform best and ignore the rest. The underutilized

experts get no gradient signal and never improve. The model is a MoE in structure but a tiny dense model in practice.

The auxiliary load balancing loss is the standard solution but adds training

complexity. Too much auxiliary loss and routing optimization dominates. Too

little and collapse happens anyway.

TRAINING INSTABILITY

MoE models are notoriously more unstable during training than equivalent

dense models. Loss spikes, NaN values, and divergence are more common.

This is partly because the routing decisions create discrete jumps in the

computational graph that do not behave smoothly.

Careful tuning of learning rates, gradient clipping, expert capacity, and

router initialization is required. Many practitioners use lower learning rates and more aggressive gradient clipping for MoE than for dense models of

similar capability.

PointWhat it means
Token droppingWhen an expert receives more tokens than its capacity buffer, excess tokens are dropped (processed with only the residual connection, no expert computation). During training, high drop rates mean some tokens receive no expert processing, which degrades gradient quality and final model quality. Managing expert capacity requires careful calibration.
Communication overhead in distributed trainingThe all-to-all communication required to route tokens to experts across devices adds latency to each training step. In large clusters, this communication can become a significant bottleneck. Efficient implementation with proper overlapping of computation and communication is required to achieve good hardware utilization.

Q09 How do transformers learn contextual token relationships during training?

This is really asking about what gradient descent discovers in the attention and FFN weights that enables contextual understanding.

ATTENTION WEIGHT LEARNING

The query, key, and value weight matrices (Wq, Wk, Wv) are learned

parameters. During training, the model gets feedback (gradient) about which

attention patterns led to correct predictions.

If attending to the subject token strongly when encoding a verb consistently

reduces the language modeling loss (because subject-verb agreement is

important), the model adjusts Wq and Wk so that verb queries produce high

dot products with subject keys. This is not programmed; it emerges from millions of gradient updates over the training data.

PointWhat it means
Multi-head specializationDifferent attention heads converge to different specializations because they start with different random initializations and follow different gradient paths. One head may find it useful to track grammatical subjects. Another finds tracking semantic similarity useful. The gradient descent independently optimizes each head for whatever attention pattern reduces loss most.
FFN weight learningFFN layers learn to store and retrieve knowledge. For a language modeling objective, predicting "The Eiffel Tower is located in [MASK]" correctly requires knowing that Paris follows Eiffel Tower. The FFN weights store these associations as patterns in the weight matrices.

Research has shown that specific facts can be localized to specific

FFN neurons in transformer models.

LAYER-BY-LAYER ABSTRACTION

Earlier layers learn shallower patterns (word identity, local context). Later layers learn more abstract patterns (semantic roles, factual associations).

This hierarchy emerges naturally from the depth of the network and the

training objective.

Q10 How do residual connections and layer normalization stabilize transformer training, and how do Pre-LN and Post-LN architectures differ?

Residual connections provide a direct path for gradients during backpropagation, preventing gradient vanishing in deep networks.

INSTEAD OF COMPUTING

output = LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x))

MODERN TRANSFORMERS TYPICALLY USE PRE-LAYERNORM

output = x + Sublayer(LayerNorm(x))

WHY PRE-LN BECAME STANDARD

Better gradient stability in very deep models

Enables training transformers with 100+ layers

REDUCES TRAINING DIVERGENCE

Post-LN (original Transformer paper) works but becomes unstable in very deep architectures.

RESIDUAL CONNECTIONS ENSURE

OUTPUT = X + F(X)

This gives a direct gradient path of 1, preventing gradient shrinkage across

many layers.

Layer normalization stabilizes training by normalizing feature activations across the hidden dimension for each token.

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q11 How does expert routing affect model efficiency and training stability?

Expert routing is the mechanism that determines both the efficiency gains (good routing means efficient computation) and

stability risks (bad routing means training problems).

EFFICIENCY DEPENDS ON ROUTING QUALITY

If the router consistently sends related tokens to the same experts, those

experts develop specialized knowledge. A routing pattern where medical tokens go to expert 3 and legal tokens go to expert 7 allows those experts to

develop domain specialization.

This specialization improves performance and efficiency: each expert becomes very good at its domain with fewer parameters than a generalist expert of the same size.

If routing is random or uniform (every expert sees everything equally), no specialization happens and you lose much of the MoE benefit.

THE DISCRETE ROUTING PROBLEM

Hard routing (assign each token to exactly K experts) creates a non-

differentiable operation. The router makes a discrete selection that cannot be differentiated with respect to the router's weights in the standard sense.

This is why the auxiliary load balancing loss is applied in the continuous

routing probability space rather than the discrete selection.

Some approaches use soft routing (weighted combination of all experts for each token) during training and hard routing during inference. This improves

gradient flow but increases training compute.

STABILITY THROUGH CAPACITY FACTORS

The expert capacity factor (how many tokens each expert can process per

batch relative to an equal distribution) is a key hyperparameter. Too tight (capacity factor 1.0) and any imbalance causes token dropping and

instability. Too loose (capacity factor 2.0) wastes memory and compute.

Most practitioners use capacity factors between 1.0 and 2.0 with

monitoring of token drop rates throughout training.

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q12 What problem does the Mixture of Experts architecture attempt to solve in large transformer models?

The core problem is that dense transformer models use all their parameters for every input token, which becomes inefficient and

expensive as you scale to hundreds of billions of parameters.

THE SCALING CHALLENGE

A 100-billion-parameter dense model processes every input token through

all 100 billion parameters. The computation cost scales directly with parameter count. Doubling parameters doubles computation, doubles

memory, doubles cost.

But do you really need all 100 billion parameters to process every token? A

simple common word like "the" probably does not need the same computation as a complex technical term. A question about cooking does

not need the same knowledge as a question about quantum physics.

WHAT MOE DOES DIFFERENTLY

MoE replaces the single FFN in each transformer block with multiple "expert"

FFNs (typically 8, 16, or 64 experts). A lightweight routing network selects only 1 or 2 experts to process each token. The other experts are completely

skipped.

This means a model with 8 experts, each the size of a standard FFN, has 8x

the total parameters but only activates 1/8 of them per token. Total parameter count is large (enabling knowledge storage) but computation per

token stays approximately constant.

THE PRACTICAL PAYOFF

Mixtral 8x7B has 46 billion total parameters but only activates about 13

billion per token. It achieves performance competitive with much larger dense models while matching the inference speed of a 13B dense model. You

get the knowledge capacity of a large model with the computation cost of a

small model.

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q13 Why do transformers require positional encoding to process sequences?

This is a subtle but important point. Without positional encoding, a transformer is completely order-blind.

THE PERMUTATION INVARIANCE PROBLEM

Self-attention computes relationships based entirely on the content of

token embeddings. It does not look at positions anywhere in its

computation. The attention score between "cat" and "sat" is the same whether "cat" comes before "sat" or after "sat".

If you shuffled all the tokens in a sentence and fed it to a transformer

without positional encoding, you would get the exact same output

representations (just shuffled the same way). The model cannot distinguish "dog bites man" from "man bites dog".

THE FIX: INJECT POSITION INFORMATION

Positional encoding adds a position-specific vector to each token

embedding before the first transformer layer. Token 1 gets embedding +

position_vector_1. Token 2 gets embedding + position_vector_2. Now the input vectors encode both meaning and position.

Because the attention mechanism operates on these enriched vectors, the

attention scores now implicitly incorporate positional information. "cat" at position 1 has a slightly different vector than "cat" at position 10, so their

interaction patterns with surrounding tokens will differ.

TWO MAIN APPROACHES

Absolute positional encoding adds fixed (sinusoidal) or learned vectors to

each position. Simple and effective for sequences up to the training length.

Relative positional encoding encodes the distance between tokens rather than their absolute positions. More generalizable to sequences

longer than seen during training. RoPE is the most popular modern variant.

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q15 Can you explain the full transformer architecture from input embeddings to output predictions?

Sure, let me walk through it step by step the way it actually works during a forward pass.

PointWhat it means
Step 1: Tokenization and embeddingThe raw text first gets tokenized into sub-word tokens. Each token is mapped to an integer ID, and that ID is looked up in an embedding matrix to get a dense vector, typically 512 or 768 dimensions depending on the model. So the input becomes a matrix of shape (sequence length x embedding dimension).
Step 2: Positional encodingSince the transformer has no built-in sense of order, we add positional information directly to the token embeddings. Each position gets a unique positional vector added to it, so the model can distinguish "cat sat" from "sat cat".

The result is still a (sequence length x embedding dimension)

matrix, but now each vector encodes both meaning and position.

STEP 3: TRANSFORMER BLOCKS

This is where the real work happens. The enriched embeddings pass through N identical transformer blocks stacked on top of each other. Each block

contains:

A self-attention sublayer where every token looks at every other

token and updates its representation based on what is relevant

A feed-forward sublayer that applies a small MLP independently to

each token position

Residual connections that add the input of each sublayer back to its output

PointWhat it means
Layer normalization that stabilizes the scale of activationsAfter each block, the representations get richer and more contextual. Token representations in deeper layers encode more abstract, task-relevant information.
Step 4: Final outputFor a language model, the final block's output (sequence length x embedding dimension) is projected through a linear layer to vocabulary size, giving a (sequence length x vocab size) matrix of logits. Softmax converts these to probabilities over the vocabulary.

The token with the highest probability at the last position is the

next predicted token.

For classification tasks like BERT, just the CLS token representation from the final layer is passed to a small classifier head.

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q16 Why does the transformer architecture rely entirely on attention instead of recurrence or convolution?

This is a really good question because it gets at the core design philosophy of transformers.

THE PROBLEM WITH RECURRENCE (RNNS)

RNNs process tokens one at a time. To get from token 1 to token 100,

information has to travel through 99 sequential steps. Two problems happen

here. First, the gradient has to backpropagate through all those steps, which causes vanishing gradients. Second, you cannot parallelize training because

step N depends on step N-1.

For long sequences this is both slow and lossy.

PointWhat it means
The problem with convolution (CNNs)CNNs apply fixed-size kernels that look at local neighborhoods. A kernel of size 3 sees 3 adjacent tokens. To model dependencies between tokens that are far apart, you need many stacked layers, and even then the receptive field grows slowly. They are great for local patterns but awkward for long-range relationships.
Why attention solves both problemsAttention computes a direct relationship between every pair of tokens in a single operation. Token 1 and token 100 have the same connection cost as tokens 1 and 2. There is no distance penalty. And the entire attention matrix is computed in parallel as a single matrix multiplication, so training is dramatically faster.

The practical payoff is huge. A model trained on GPUs can process the entire

sequence simultaneously rather than sequentially. And a word at position 1

can directly influence the representation of a word at position 500 without

degradation. That is simply not possible with RNNs at scale.

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q17 How does causal language modeling differ from masked language modeling?

These two objectives represent fundamentally different approaches to self-supervised training on text, and they produce models with

different strengths.

CAUSAL LANGUAGE MODELING (GPT-STYLE)

The model predicts each token from all previous tokens. Given "The cat sat

on the", predict "mat". Given "The cat sat on the mat", predict the next token. The training loss is the average cross-entropy over every position in the

sequence.

The attention mask is causal: each position only attends to prior positions. This is necessary to prevent the model from looking ahead and trivially solving the task.

This objective trains the model to be a good text generator. It learns the full joint distribution of text by learning to continue any prefix.

MASKED LANGUAGE MODELING (BERT-STYLE)

Randomly mask 15% of tokens, replacing them with a special [MASK] token.

The model predicts the masked tokens from the surrounding unmasked context. The training loss is computed only on the masked positions.

The attention mask is fully bidirectional: each position can attend to all other

unmasked positions. This allows the model to use both left and right context

when predicting a masked token.

This objective trains the model to understand context deeply. Predicting a masked word requires understanding what word makes sense given everything around it.

KEY PRACTICAL DIFFERENCES

CLM trains on 100% of tokens in every sequence (every position is a

prediction target). MLM trains on only about 15% (only the masked positions). CLM is more sample-efficient from this perspective.

CLM produces autoregressive generative models. MLM produces bidirectional understanding models. They optimize for different end goals.

A INTERVIEW-READY ANSWER

Q18 What architectural role does the feed-forward network play inside a transformer block?

A lot of people focus entirely on attention and treat the feed-forward network as an afterthought. That is a mistake. The FFN plays

a distinct and important role.

WHAT THE FFN ACTUALLY DOES

After self-attention, each token has a new representation that incorporates

information from other tokens in the sequence. The FFN then processes each of those representations independently. It applies the same two-layer

MLP to every position:

Linear layer: expands from model dimension (say 768) to 4x that

3072)

Non-linear activation (GELU or ReLU)

Linear layer: projects back down to model dimension (768)

PointWhat it means
Why the 4x expansion mattersThe expansion creates a higher-dimensional space where the model can perform more complex non-linear transformations before projecting back. Think of it like working in a larger workspace, doing computation there, then summarizing back to the original dimension.
Attention vs FFN: what each doesSelf-attention is fundamentally about routing: it figures out which other tokens are relevant and mixes their information together. It answers the question "what context should I incorporate?"

The FFN is about computation on the result: given this contextualized

representation, what transformation should I apply? Research has shown

that FFN layers store factual knowledge. When you see a model completing

The Eiffel Tower is located in..." the FFN layers are where the stored association "Eiffel Tower: Paris" gets activated.

This is why removing FFN layers significantly degrades factual recall even when attention layers are kept intact.

Q19 What architectural differences exist between MoE transformers and dense transformers?

The differences are mostly contained to the FFN sublayer within each transformer block.

PointWhat it means
Dense transformer FFNEvery transformer block has one FFN: a two-layer MLP with expand-activate-project. All tokens pass through this FFN in every forward pass. Parameters: 2 times (d_model times d_ffn).
MoE transformer FFNThe single FFN is replaced by N expert FFNs plus a router network. Each expert has the same structure as a standard FFN. The router adds a small linear layer (d_model times N_experts). Only top-K experts activate per token.

Total FFN parameters: N times 2 times (d_model times d_ffn_expert). But compute per token: K times 2 times (d_model times d_ffn_expert) where K is much less than N.

PointWhat it means
Everything else stays the sameThe attention mechanism, positional encoding, residual connections, layer normalization, and embedding layers are identical to dense transformers. Only the FFN sublayer changes.
Distributed training requires expert parallelismIn a dense model, you shard the model by layers (pipeline parallelism) or by matrices (tensor parallelism). In a MoE model, experts naturally distribute across devices. Different GPUs or nodes host different experts.

The routing step requires an all-to-all communication operation to

send each token to its assigned expert, which may be on a different device.
